## The short answer

<Evidence level="fact" /> After Singapore fell to Japan in February 1942, many of the Allied
prisoners taken there were shipped elsewhere as forced labour. A group of Australian and British
POWs was sent to Sandakan, in North Borneo, to build a Japanese military airfield. The Australian
War Memorial's own research guide puts the total at 2,500 Australian and British prisoners of war
held there over the course of the war.

<Evidence level="fact" /> As Japan's position collapsed, two forced marches sent the prisoners
still alive at Sandakan inland toward Ranau — the first in January 1945, the second in May 1945 —
staggering roughly 260 kilometres along jungle tracks. Of the 2,500 held at Sandakan, only six men
survived: all six Australian, and all six survived specifically because they escaped during the
march, not because they endured it to the end. Those too weak to march were left behind at
Sandakan, where the Australian War Memorial states plainly that all of them — Australian and
British alike — died or were killed.

## Why it happened

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> The marches weren't a single deliberate massacre order so much
as the endpoint of a camp regime that had already been killing prisoners for years through
starvation, disease, beatings and forced labour, combined with a retreating Japanese garrison that
had no intention of leaving prisoners alive or capable of being liberated as Allied forces closed
in on Borneo. The exact command decisions behind the marches are the kind of detail this article
still needs a properly cited primary or scholarly source for, rather than a plausible-sounding
guess.

## Why this still matters

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> Sandakan is one of the darkest and least publicly known
Australian and British experiences of the Pacific War — heavier Australian losses than any other
single episode of the war, in a place most people outside Sabah and Australia have never heard of.
It's also a reminder that Sabah's WWII history isn't a footnote to Singapore's or Malaya's: the
camp existed *because* of what happened in Singapore in 1942, but what happened afterward, in
Sandakan and on the road to Ranau, is its own story, centred on North Borneo.

## What people often get wrong

- Treating Sandakan as a footnote to the Fall of Singapore, rather than its own distinct atrocity.
- Assuming the death toll came only from the marches themselves — conditions at the camp before the marches began were already killing prisoners.
