## The short answer

"Malaysia became independent in 1957" is a sentence you'll hear constantly, and it's wrong in a
way that matters. <Evidence level="fact" /> On 31 August 1957, the **Federation of Malaya** — the
Malay Peninsula states only — became independent from Britain. Sabah, Sarawak, Singapore and
Brunei were not part of it. Malaysia, as a country including those territories, didn't exist yet.

<Evidence level="fact" /> Malaysia was formed over six years later, on 16 September 1963, when
the Federation of Malaya merged with Singapore, North Borneo (renamed Sabah) and Sarawak under
the Malaysia Agreement signed in London on 9 July 1963. Brunei, originally intended as a fifth
member, did not join.

## Two ways to describe 1963 — and why neither is the whole story

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> Politically, it's fair to describe 1963 as Malaya, Sabah,
Sarawak and Singapore **forming Malaysia together** — a new federation bargained into existence,
not simply Malaya expanding outward.

<Evidence level="fact" /> But at the United Nations, the picture is different. The UN's own
member-state record states that the Federation of Malaya joined the UN on 17 September 1957, and
that *"its name was changed to Malaysia"* on 16 September 1963, following the admission of
Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak into the federation. Internationally, this was recorded as a
continuation of the same UN membership under a new name — not the dissolution of one state and
the admission of a new one.

Both of these are legitimate ways to read 1963. An article that picks only one and presents it as
the whole truth is flattening a genuinely disputed point of framing — which is exactly what this
site tries not to do.

## Three dates, and the one that trips people up

<Evidence level="fact" /> There are three dates worth holding separately in your head:

- **31 August 1957** — Merdeka: the Federation of Malaya's actual independence.
- **31 August 1963** — North Borneo moved into internal self-government, with Donald Stephens as
  Chief Minister. This date is often described in public discourse as Sabah's "independence day."
- **16 September 1963** — Malaysia Day: the Malaysia Agreement takes effect, British sovereignty
  over North Borneo formally ends, and Malaysia is formed.

That two of these three dates fall on the same calendar day, six years apart, is a large part of
why "Malaya" and "Malaysia" get collapsed into one story.

<Evidence level="fact" /> The gap between them isn't a grey area — the treaty itself is explicit
about it. Article IV of the Malaysia Agreement provides for Britain to relinquish "sovereignty and
jurisdiction in respect of North Borneo, Sarawak and Singapore" **as from Malaysia Day**, and the
Sabah State Constitution annexed to the agreement is written throughout around "Malaysia Day" —
16 September — as the point it takes legal effect, not 31 August. Whatever happened in North
Borneo on 31 August 1963 (Donald Stephens sworn in, internal self-government) took place under
separate British colonial constitutional machinery; sovereignty itself, and the Sabah State
Constitution that made it a state of Malaysia, only came into force on 16 September.

## Who signed, and who didn't — and a deadline that moved three days out

<Evidence level="fact" /> The Malaysia Agreement was signed in London on 9 July 1963 by the United
Kingdom, the Federation of Malaya, North Borneo, Sarawak and Singapore. Brunei is not a signatory.

Here's a detail that doesn't make it into most retellings: **31 August 1963 wasn't a soft target
that slipped — it was written into the treaty as Malaysia Day**, in Article II of the agreement as
signed on 9 July. It only became 16 September because of a second, short treaty: an amending
agreement signed at **Singapore on 28 August 1963** — three days before the original date — which
simply substitutes "16th September" for "31st August" in Article II. It's signed by Duncan Sandys
for the UK, Abdul Razak for the Federation of Malaya, **Donald Stephens for North Borneo**, S. K.
Ningkan for Sarawak, and Goh Keng Swee for Singapore.

<Evidence level="fact" /> The reason for that last-minute change was a United Nations mission
assessing opinion in Sabah and Sarawak, after Indonesia and the Philippines objected to the process
that had preceded it (the Cobbold Commission). That UN mission is its own story, with its own
disputes, and will get its own article rather than a rushed summary here.

## Why this isn't a stable, one-time settlement

<Evidence level="fact" /> Singapore, one of the four founding members of Malaysia, separated from
it on 9 August 1965 — less than two years after joining. Brunei never joined at all. Of the
territories discussed in the 1961–63 Malaysia proposal, only Malaya, Sabah and Sarawak ended up in
a federation that has lasted to today in that form. "1963" wasn't a single finished event; it was
the start of a federation that immediately kept changing shape.

## What people often get wrong

- Treating 1957 "Merdeka" and 1963 "Malaysia Day" as the same anniversary.
- Describing "Malaysia" as simply Malaya's later name, with nothing else having changed.
- Assuming Sabah's 31 August 1963 self-government date meant the same thing as full independence.
