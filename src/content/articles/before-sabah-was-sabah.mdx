## The short answer

<Evidence level="fact" /> Sabah did not begin as a single, clean handover. It began as a
commercial land grab by two private individuals — Gustavus Baron de Overbeck, an Austrian
businessman based in Hong Kong, and Alfred Dent, a London merchant — who spent 1877 and 1878
collecting grants of territory and trading rights from two different, and not entirely compatible,
sources of authority: the Sultanate of Brunei and the Sultanate of Sulu.

<Evidence level="fact" /> Both sultanates claimed authority over overlapping stretches of what is
now Sabah's east coast. Rather than settling that question first, Overbeck and Dent simply
obtained grants from both. The grants themselves are the clearest primary evidence that Sabah's
underlying sovereignty was contested from the very beginning of this story, not just in later
political arguments.

## The overlapping grants

<Evidence level="fact" /> On 29 December 1877, Sultan Abdul Mumin of Brunei granted Overbeck and
Dent several packages of territory, including — in the words of the grant itself — "the States of
Paitan, Sugut, Bangaya, Labuk, Sandakan, Kina Batangan, Mumiang, and all the territories as far as
the Sibuco River." In exchange, Overbeck and Dent agreed to pay Brunei two thousand dollars a year.
The grant included a specific condition: the Sultan could take the territory back if that payment
lapsed for three consecutive years.

<Evidence level="fact" /> Four weeks later, on 22 January 1878, Sultan Mohammad Jamalul Alam of
Sulu granted Overbeck and Dent — the same two men — territory described as running from "the
Pandassan River on the north-west coast" along the entire east coast "as far as the Sibuco River,"
naming several of the identical states Brunei had already granted: Paitan, Sugut, Bangaya, Labuk,
Sandakan, Kina Batangan, Mumiang. This time the payment was five thousand dollars a year, and the
wording was stronger: the Sultan and his Datus agreed "to grant and cede... for ever and in
perpetuity all the rights and powers belonging to us over" the territory — with no reversion clause
if payment stopped.

<Evidence level="disputed" /> Two different sultans, four weeks apart, granting much of the same
land under different terms and different prices, is not a historical footnote — it's the origin of
a dispute that is still politically live today (see "Why this still matters," below). This site
will treat the Philippines' modern sovereignty claim to Sabah, which traces back to the Sulu side
of this story, in its own dedicated article rather than resolve it here.

## Building a company out of grants

<Evidence level="fact" /> Brunei had granted Overbeck and Dent still more territory the same week
in December 1877: the Gaya Bay and Sapangar Bay area on the west coast (four thousand dollars a
year), and a separate block running from the Sulaman River to the Paitan River covering twenty-one
named states (six thousand dollars a year) — both, like the Sibuco grant, containing a clause
letting the Sultan reclaim the land if payments lapsed for three years. All of this is recited in
the Crown's own words in the 1881 Royal Charter, which lists each grant individually before
confirming the new company's authority over them.

<Evidence level="fact" /> On 1 November 1881, Queen Victoria's government issued a Royal Charter
incorporating the British North Borneo Company, built from Overbeck and Dent's collected grants,
commissions and cessions. A chartered company — a private, profit-seeking corporation — was now
the entity governing the territory, not the British Crown directly. This is one of Sabah's more
unusual features: for decades, "the government" of North Borneo was a London-registered company
with shareholders.

<Evidence level="fact" /> On 12 May 1888, Britain and the Company signed a Protectorate Agreement.
Article I set out the Company's territory, organised into nine named provinces (Alcock, Cunliffe,
Dent, Dewhurst, Elphinstone, Keppel, Martin, Mayne and Mayburgh). Article II is the important part:
North Borneo would "continue to be governed and administered as an independent State by the
Company," now simply under British protection — meaning Britain took responsibility for North
Borneo's foreign relations and defence, while the Company kept running the place day to day. This
protectorate-plus-company arrangement lasted until the Japanese occupation in 1942 and the
territory's transition to a Crown Colony in 1946.

## Why it happened

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> By the 1870s, both Brunei and Sulu were sultanates under
pressure — Brunei had already lost significant territory to the Brooke family's Sarawak to its
south, and Sulu faced growing Spanish military pressure from the Philippines to its north.
European trading companies were actively looking for footholds in Borneo, following patterns
already set in Sarawak and the Dutch East Indies. Overbeck and Dent's project fit that wider
pattern of European commercial expansion filling space that weakening regional powers could no
longer fully defend or administer. The specific commercial calculations behind Overbeck and Dent's
venture, and exactly how much either sultanate understood about what they were granting, are the
kind of detail this article still needs a properly cited scholarly source for, rather than a
plausible-sounding guess.

## A story people love to oversimplify: how did Sulu even have land to grant?

<Evidence level="legend" /> A popular story holds that Brunei ceded territory in northeastern
Borneo to the Sultanate of Sulu in the 17th century, as a reward for Sulu's help during a Bruneian
civil war. This story is widely repeated, but its details and historical reliability are disputed
among historians — it should not be treated as settled fact without stronger scholarship than this
site currently has on hand. Whatever the truth of that older story, by the 1870s, Sulu clearly
asserted — and acted on — a claim of authority over part of the same coastline Brunei also claimed,
which is precisely what the overlapping 1877–78 grants document.

## Why this still matters

<Evidence level="political" /> The modern Philippine government's claim to Sabah traces its
legal argument back to the Sulu side of this story — the position that the Sultanate of Sulu never
fully ceded sovereignty in 1878, only leased it, and that those rights eventually passed to the
Philippine state. Malaysia's position is that the 1878 instrument, on its own English-translated
text, reads as a cession ("grant and cede... for ever and in perpetuity"), later exercised as
effective sovereignty and confirmed by Sabah's inclusion in Malaysia in 1963. Both of these are
serious legal-political positions, not simple facts, and this site will address the live dispute
properly in its own article rather than adjudicate it here.

## What people often get wrong

- Treating British North Borneo as a straightforward single cession from one clear sovereign, when the founding documents themselves show two competing grantors.
- Assuming "protectorate" in 1888 meant Britain took over day-to-day government — Article II of the 1888 Agreement explicitly kept the Company in charge of administration.
- Stating the Brunei-to-Sulu 17th-century transfer story as established history rather than a disputed tradition.
