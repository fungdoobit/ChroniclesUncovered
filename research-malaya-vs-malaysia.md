## 1. The Core Distinction (established fact)

- **31 August 1957**: The **Federation of Malaya** becomes independent from Britain. Territory: the Malay Peninsula states only. Legal basis: the Federation of Malaya Agreement 1957 / Federation of Malaya Independence Act 1957, built on a constitution drafted by the Reid Commission.
- **16 September 1963**: The **Federation of Malaysia** is formed, merging the existing Federation of Malaya with Singapore, North Borneo (Sabah), and Sarawak, under the Malaysia Agreement (MA63) signed in London on 9 July 1963.
- Brunei was originally intended as a fifth member and never joined.
- These are two separate treaties and two different sets of territories — the factual spine of your article's title.

**[added from HISTORY_RESEARCH.md, verified via primary source]** — the "new country" framing needs one important qualifier: **international-legal continuity**. I fetched the UN's own member-states page directly, and it states plainly: *"The Federation of Malaya joined the United Nations on 17 September 1957. On 16 September 1963, its name was changed to Malaysia, following the admission to the new federation of Singapore, Sabah (North Borneo) and Sarawak."* (un.org/en/about-us/member-states/malaysia). So at the UN, this wasn't recorded as Malaya dissolving and a new state being admitted — it's treated as the same UN membership, renamed. This is exactly the kind of thing `CLAUDE.md` warns against flattening: politically and territorially, 1963 created something new; internationally/legally, there's a continuity argument too. The article should present both readings rather than picking one, per your own editorial rule #4.

## 2. What Happened Before 1957

- Malayan Union (1946) → Federation of Malaya (1948), against the backdrop of the Malayan Emergency (communist insurgency).
- 1955: the Alliance (UMNO-MCA-MIC) wins Malaya's first general election under Tunku Abdul Rahman.
- January–February 1956: London Conference. Britain agrees in principle to independence "by August 1957 if possible."
- 1956: Reid Commission appointed to draft the constitution for an independent Malaya.
- 31 August 1957: Merdeka. Tunku Abdul Rahman proclaims independence at Merdeka Stadium, Kuala Lumpur.

**Note for the article**: Malaya's independence was a relatively clean bilateral handover between one colony and Britain. This is the contrast point for everything that follows.

## 3. From Independence to "Malaysia" (1957–1961)

- **27 May 1961**: Tunku Abdul Rahman publicly proposes a wider federation — "Malaysia" — combining Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo, Sarawak, and Brunei.
- **Interpretation, not settled fact** — commonly cited motives (each should be treated as historians' analysis, not proven intent):
  - Counterbalancing a Chinese-majority Singapore joining Malaya alone, by adding the more ethnically mixed Borneo territories.
  - Accelerating British decolonisation of Sabah/Sarawak/Brunei on terms favourable to Malaya rather than leaving them to go independent separately or fall toward Indonesia.
  - Concerns (British and Malayan) about communist influence in Singapore.
  - Regional defence and Commonwealth strategic considerations as Britain reduced its military footprint East of Suez.

## 4. Testing "the Will of the People": Two Commissions

Because Sabah and Sarawak were still British colonies, not independent actors choosing to merge, their consent had to be assessed externally. This happened twice — and both processes are contested.

### Cobbold Commission (1962)
- Established January 1962, chaired by Lord Cobbold; also included representatives from Malaya and former colonial administrators.
- Toured North Borneo and Sarawak; received roughly 2,200 written memoranda.
- Findings (as commonly summarised): opinion split roughly into thirds — enthusiastic support, conditional support (pending safeguards), and a mix of opposition or a preference for independence before any merger.
- Recommended the federation proceed, with negotiated safeguards for the Borneo states (the basis of the later "20-point" and "18-point" agreements — **not yet researched in detail; flagged below**).
- **Disputed / contested interpretation**: present-day Sabah/Sarawak rights commentators (e.g. the advocacy site Republic of Sabah North Borneo) argue the Commission was not genuinely representative — one claim is that only around 4,000 people were interviewed to represent a population exceeding a million, and that no formal referendum was ever held. This is a live political grievance today, not a neutral historical consensus — present it as a contested view, sourced to who's making it.

### Manila Accord and UN Malaysia Mission (1963)
- Indonesia and the Philippines rejected the Cobbold process as insufficiently neutral (Malaya-influenced) and opposed the merger.
- **31 July 1963**: Malaya, Indonesia, and the Philippines sign the **Manila Accord**, agreeing that UN Secretary-General U Thant would independently verify the wishes of the Sabah/Sarawak population, per UN General Assembly Resolution 1541 (XV).
- August–September 1963: the **UN Malaysia Mission**, led by Laurence Michelmore, conducts hearings in Sabah and Sarawak.
- **14 September 1963**: U Thant's final report concludes there was clear majority support for joining Malaysia.
- **Genuine procedural dispute, documented by U Thant himself**: on 29 August 1963 — before the UN report was published — Tunku announced Malaysia would be formed on 16 September. U Thant's own report noted this was premature and caused "misunderstanding, confusion, and even resentment" among the other Manila Accord parties. Indonesia and the Philippines used this to argue Malaya had breached the spirit of the Manila Accord; Malaya and Britain maintained the outcome was unaffected. This is a good example of a fact (the announcement predated the report) with a disputed interpretation attached (whether it invalidated the process).

## 5. Who Signed the Malaysia Agreement — and Who Didn't

- Signed London, 9 July 1963. Signatories: United Kingdom, Federation of Malaya, North Borneo, Sarawak, Singapore.
- Notable absence: **Brunei**. No Brunei signature appears on MA63.
- Effective 16 September 1963 — now Malaysia Day.
- The Agreement's annexes included interim constitutions for Sabah, Sarawak, and Singapore, and safeguards on matters like immigration, land, native customs, and (for Singapore) education and labour policy, revenue contribution, and a phased common market.
- **Contested legacy point (needs its own dedicated research later)**: some Sabah-based commentary argues MA63's actual text was brief (reportedly ~4 pages plus annexes) and that promised safeguards were thinner than assumed — feeding into present-day "MA63 rights" political debates in Sabah/Sarawak. Flag this as a live contemporary political position, not something to assert as settled history.

### 5a. The other 31 August: Sabah's own self-government date **[added from HISTORY_RESEARCH.md — good hook, needs its own primary-source check]**

Per `HISTORY_RESEARCH.md`: on **31 August 1963**, North Borneo moved into internal self-government, with Donald Stephens as Chief Minister — sometimes described in public discourse as Sabah's "independence day." This is a genuinely careful point to get right: British sovereignty over North Borneo didn't formally end until the Malaysia arrangements took effect on 16 September 1963. So there are now *three* dates in play that are easy to conflate:

- 31 Aug 1957 — Merdeka (Malaya's actual independence)
- 31 Aug 1963 — North Borneo's internal self-government (not full sovereignty)
- 16 Sept 1963 — Malaysia Day (British sovereignty formally ends; Malaysia formed)

That two of the three land on the same calendar date (31 August, six years apart) is a strong, underused detail for the article — and a good candidate for your "What people often get wrong" feature. Needs a primary source confirming Donald Stephens's exact title/date and the precise constitutional status of North Borneo between 31 Aug and 16 Sept 1963 before it's stated as settled fact (this is item 3 on your own `HISTORY_RESEARCH.md` verification checklist).

## 6. Why Brunei Stayed Out

Best summarised as an entangled mix of a revolt, a financial dispute, and a security guarantee — not a single cause.

- **December 1962: Brunei Revolt.** The Parti Rakyat Brunei (PRB), led by A.M. Azahari, launched an armed uprising (via its military wing, TNKU) after the Sultan blocked the elected, anti-Malaysia-leaning Legislative Council from convening. British troops (including Gurkhas) suppressed it within days.
- The revolt exposed genuine internal opposition to joining Malaysia and became one of the sparks for Indonesia's later Konfrontasi.
- **Important correction, from HISTORY_RESEARCH.md**: don't present the revolt itself as the reason Brunei stayed out. Brunei continued negotiating over Malaysia for another seven months after the revolt was crushed — it was the unresolved oil-revenue and sovereignty disputes of mid-1963 that were decisive, not the revolt. The revolt is context and one contributing pressure, not the cause.
- **Oil revenue dispute**: In June 1963, Malaysia's Deputy PM Abdul Razak sent the Sultan a list of unresolved issues, centred on how much control Brunei would retain over its oil wealth. The Sultan wouldn't concede enough control to satisfy Kuala Lumpur's terms.
- **British reassurance reduced the incentive to join**: after suppressing the revolt, Britain continued stationing troops in Brunei and made clear its security guarantee wasn't conditional on Brunei joining Malaysia — removing one of the main strategic reasons to federate.
- Result: Brunei remained a separate British protectorate, eventually gaining full independence in 1984 — outside Malaysia.

## 7. Why Indonesia and the Philippines Objected

### Indonesia — Konfrontasi (1963–1966)
- Sukarno framed Malaysia's formation as a British "neo-colonial" manoeuvre — a way to keep Western influence in the region under the guise of decolonisation.
- Indonesia refused to recognise the UN Mission's findings, broke diplomatic relations after Malaysia's formation, and waged an undeclared border war (mostly in Borneo) from 1963–66.
- **Interpretation, not proven fact**: several historians argue Konfrontasi also served Sukarno's domestic political needs — a unifying external enemy at a time of economic mismanagement and internal tension between the military and the Indonesian Communist Party (PKI). Present both the stated ideological justification and this more skeptical reading, clearly labelled as differing interpretations.

### The Philippines — the Sabah/Sulu claim
- The Philippines' claim rests on inheriting the rights of the Sultanate of Sulu, based on an 1878–79 agreement between the Sultanate and agents who later formed the British North Borneo Chartered Company.
- **The actual legal dispute**: Manila argues the historical agreement was a *lease* (sovereignty retained by Sulu, inherited by the Philippines); Malaysia (and the UK before it) treats it as a *full cession* of sovereignty, later exercised as effective British rule and internationally recognised when Sabah joined Malaysia in 1963.
- This is not resolved — it resurfaces periodically (notably the 2013 Lahad Datu standoff, and reportedly again as recently as 2025) and should be presented as an active, unresolved legal/political position on both sides, not adjudicated by the article.

## 8. The Aftermath That Complicates the "1963" Story: Singapore Leaves (1965)

- Singapore joined Malaysia in 1963 as part of the same merger.
- Tensions between the PAP (Singapore's ruling party) and UMNO (dominant in the federal Alliance government) surfaced almost immediately — disputes over a common market, revenue contributions, and fundamentally different visions of politics: PAP's "Malaysian Malaysia" (race-neutral) versus UMNO's communal power-sharing model.
- Racial riots broke out in Singapore in July and September 1964.
- June 1965: Tunku, in London for a Commonwealth conference, decides separation is the only option and has Deputy PM Razak lay the groundwork.
- **9 August 1965**: Singapore is separated from Malaysia and becomes independent — by most accounts, effectively expelled rather than seceding voluntarily, though Singaporean finance minister Goh Keng Swee's own suggestion of parting ways is described as the proximate trigger for the final negotiations.
- **Why this matters for your article**: it demonstrates that the 1963 federation was not a stable, finished settlement the way 1957 was — one of its founding members left within two years. That's a strong closing beat for a piece titled "Malaya Was Not Malaysia."
