## The short answer

<Evidence level="fact" /> When Tunku Abdul Rahman publicly proposed a wider "Malaysia" federation
on 27 May 1961, Brunei was one of the five territories in the plan, alongside Malaya, Singapore,
North Borneo and Sarawak. Brunei never signed the Malaysia Agreement. It remained a separate
British protectorate, and did not become fully independent until 1984 — twenty-one years after
Malaysia was formed without it.

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> The popular one-line explanation is that Brunei stayed out
"because of oil" — the Sultanate had substantial petroleum revenue relative to its small
population, and didn't want to share it. This site's own research treats that explanation as too
simple. The more accurate summary: the Sultan concluded that the federation terms on offer did not
sufficiently protect Brunei's sovereignty, its finances and oil interests, and his own position as
monarch — three connected concerns, not one.

## The Brunei Revolt — a spark, not the cause

<Evidence level="fact" /> On 8 December 1962, the Parti Rakyat Brunei (PRB), led by A. M. Azahari,
launched an armed uprising through its military wing, after the Sultan blocked an elected,
anti-Malaysia-leaning Legislative Council from convening. British troops, including Gurkha units,
suppressed the revolt within days.

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> It's tempting to treat the revolt as the reason Brunei stayed
out of Malaysia, but this site's research specifically flags that as a misreading: Brunei kept
negotiating over Malaysia for another seven months after the revolt was crushed. The revolt
exposed real internal opposition to federation and became one of the sparks for Indonesia's later
Konfrontasi campaign — but the decision to stay out came later, over unresolved financial and
sovereignty questions in mid-1963, not from the revolt itself.

## What actually broke the negotiations

<Evidence level="fact" /> In June 1963, Malaysia's Deputy Prime Minister Abdul Razak sent the
Sultan a list of unresolved issues, centred on how much control Brunei would retain over its own
oil wealth. The Sultan was not willing to concede enough control to satisfy Kuala Lumpur's terms.

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> Money wasn't the only issue in play, and arguably wasn't even
the deepest one. The Sultan's precedence within the federation's Conference of Rulers became a
genuinely contentious point — not simply ceremonial, but a proxy for a bigger question: how much
real sovereignty and authority would the Sultan keep once Brunei sat inside a federation run by
new federal institutions in Kuala Lumpur? Academic work on Brunei's side of this story emphasises
resistance to diluting Brunei's sovereignty as the central thread, with oil and precedence as the
concrete issues that sovereignty concern actually showed up in.

<Evidence level="fact" /> After suppressing the December 1962 revolt, Britain kept troops
stationed in Brunei and made clear that its security guarantee to the Sultanate did not depend on
Brunei joining Malaysia. That removed one of the strongest strategic reasons Brunei might otherwise
have had to federate — protection was available either way.

## Why this still matters

<Evidence level="interpretation" /> Brunei's path — staying out, keeping its oil revenue and
monarchy fully under its own control, and reaching full independence in 1984 as one of the
region's wealthiest small states per capita — is one of the natural comparison points for
questions about what an independent Sabah might have looked like (a separate, clearly
counterfactual question this site treats in its own article). Brunei's story shows that
"joining Malaysia" was never inevitable for any of the territories in the 1961 proposal — it was
negotiated, territory by territory, and could fail.

## What people often get wrong

- "Brunei stayed out only because of oil" — oil revenue control was one piece of a larger sovereignty dispute, not the whole story.
- Treating the December 1962 revolt as the direct cause of Brunei staying out — Brunei kept negotiating for seven more months afterward.
- Assuming Brunei's continued British protection required joining Malaysia — Britain's security guarantee held either way.
